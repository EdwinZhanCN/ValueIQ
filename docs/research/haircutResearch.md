# Haircut calibration research (ValueIQ, section 3.7)

Compiled 2026-11-04. READ = I opened and read the text (PDF text extracted with pypdf, or page fetched). SNIPPET = seen only in a search-result summary, not verified against the source.

## Summary (10 lines)

1. No primary source gives a "benefit haircut by evidence tier". Our five band edges remain team judgment; the evidence below only brackets them. State that honestly in the doc.
2. UK Green Book optimism bias (READ): Equipment/Development (includes ICT software) capex uplift is 200% upper / 10% lower; works duration 54% / 10%. Start at upper, reduce by how well risk factors are mitigated. This is the closest published analogue to "sliding by backing".
3. The same Green Book guidance (READ, sec 4.1) says there was NO data to set bounds for operating costs or benefit shortfall; appraisers should use sensitivity / switching values. So Treasury does not give a benefit haircut either.
4. Flyvbjerg and Budzier (READ, 1,471 IT projects): mean cost overrun 27%; one in six was a "black swan" with ~200% cost overrun and ~70% schedule overrun. Cost-side only; no benefit-shortfall figure.
5. Jorgensen reviews (SNIPPET): software effort/cost overruns average about 30%, 60-80% of projects overrun by 30-40%. Consistent with Flyvbjerg's 27%.
6. Nucleus C41 (READ, Feb 2007): correction factor 0.7-0.9 sales, near 1 line workers, 0.5 or less marketing; use 0.5 as an average or conservative default. This is a recapture factor, not an evidence-quality haircut. Our model already has R (recapture); do not double count.
7. Time-saved realization (READ): Danish study of 25,000 workers (Humlum and Vestergaard 2025) found self-reported savings of 2.8% of hours vs 15%+ in RCTs, no detectable earnings or hours change, only 3-7% pass-through to earnings. METR RCT (READ, 16 devs, 246 tasks): devs predicted 24% faster, were 19% slower, and still believed 20% faster. Self-reported time savings are unreliable.
8. UK Copilot trial (READ press release): 26 min/day, but self-reported, vendor-involved, extrapolated. Illustrates what a "benchmark" self-report looks like.
9. Calibration verdict: top and middle bands are defensible; benchmark band (10-25%) may be too LOW when the benchmark is a vendor/RCT time-saving figure; the 40-60% assumption band is NOT overdone given the evidence. Cost uplift of 10-15% (Forrester) looks LOW for early-stage internal software versus 27-30% mean overruns.
10. Not found: any primary study quantifying estimate error as a function of "measured vs estimated input" data quality (PMI page returned 403). Item 5 is only partly answered.

Other flags for the doc:

- The sliding mechanism (reduce from an upper bound according to how many risk factors are mitigated, mitigation factor 0 to 1) is directly how the Green Book does it (sec 3.11-3.15). Cite this as precedent for the backing score b; our checklist weights remain a team assumption.
- Cost uplift: Forrester 10-15% is below the 27-30% mean overrun for IT projects and far below the Green Book 200% unmitigated upper bound for ICT/development. Consider a tiered cost uplift too (e.g. ~15% for fixed-price/quoted, ~30% for estimated, higher for assumed), noting the Green Book lower bound is 10% for well-managed projects at contract award. This is a recommendation, not a sourced number.
- Do not double-count: Nucleus-style 0.5-0.9 recapture is the R in M-TS (default in our fixture is 0.5, matching Nucleus's "use .5 as an average or a more conservative measure"). The haircut H should reflect evidence quality only.
- Compounding product across several weak inputs can push the combined haircut well past any band; the data above (tail at 1 in 6) suggests the combined effective haircut of 70%+ is rarely justified. Consider a cap on the effective per-driver haircut.

## Per-source notes

### 1. HM Treasury, Supplementary Green Book Guidance: Optimism Bias (READ)

- URL: https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5a74dae740f0b65f61322c72/Optimism_bias.pdf (landing page: https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/green-book-supplementary-guidance-optimism-bias, published 21 April 2013, 15 pages). The landing page alone (fetched via WebFetch) does not contain the table; I downloaded the PDF and extracted text.
- Table 1, "Recommended Adjustment Ranges", p.2 of 15 (Optimism Bias %):

| Project type                                      | Works duration Upper | Lower | Capital expenditure Upper | Lower |
| ------------------------------------------------- | -------------------- | ----- | ------------------------- | ----- |
| Standard Buildings                                | 4                    | 1     | 24                        | 2     |
| Non-standard Buildings                            | 39                   | 2     | 51                        | 4     |
| Standard Civil Engineering                        | 20                   | 1     | 44                        | 3     |
| Non-standard Civil Engineering                    | 25                   | 3     | 66                        | 6     |
| Equipment/Development                             | 54                   | 10    | 200                       | 10    |
| Outsourcing                                       | N/A                  | N/A   | 41*                       | 0*    |
| *Outsourcing figure is for operating expenditure. |

- Definition: "Equipment & development projects: Projects that are concerned with the provision of equipment and/or development of software and systems (i.e. manufactured equipment, Information and Communication Technology (ICT) development projects) or leading edge projects." Outsourcing includes "ICT services".
- Method: sec 3.11 "Use the appropriate upper bound value ... as the starting value"; 3.12-3.14 reduce in proportion to a 0.0-1.0 mitigation factor per contributory factor; 3.15 ideally reduce to lower bound before contract award; 3.23 lower bound is the level to aim for with effective risk management.
- Sec 3.3: upper bounds "relate to the average historic optimism bias found at the outline business case stage for traditionally procured projects. Higher optimism bias adjustments may therefore be required at an earlier stage".
- Sec 4.1: "Due to a lack of available data, Mott MacDonald was unable to recommend sound upper and lower bound optimism bias levels for operating expenditure (except for outsourcing projects) or benefits shortfall." Use sensitivity analysis / switching values.
- Caveats: data comes from Mott MacDonald (2002) review of large UK public procurement (footnote 1), so it is old, UK public sector, large projects, and a cost/duration uplift, not a benefit haircut. The "200%" for equipment/development is an upper bound for unmitigated early-stage ICT. A newer Green Book (2022) exists; I did not fetch it.

### 2. Flyvbjerg and Budzier, "Why Your IT Project May Be Riskier Than You Think", HBR Sept 2011 (READ via arXiv/SSRN version)

- URLs: https://arxiv.org/abs/1304.0265 (PDF read at https://arxiv.org/pdf/1304.0265); SSRN https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2229735.
- Quotes: "The analysis of a sample of 1,471 IT projects showed that the average cost overrun was 27% ... Fully one in six of the projects in the sample was a Black Swan, with a cost overrun of 200%, on average, and a schedule overrun of almost 70%." Also: stress-test question about whether a firm can absorb "15% of its medium-sized tech projects ... exceed[ing] cost estimates by 200%". Sample average cost $167 million (largest $33 billion), so skewed toward large projects.
- Not found in the text I read: a median overrun, or any benefit-shortfall figure. The article is about cost and schedule.
- Caveats: large corporate/public IT projects, not small internal tools; "fat tail" so mean hides tail; the Standish report was not opened. A 2025 Project Management Journal paper by the same group ("The Uniqueness of IT Cost Risk", 23 project types, reportedly 11,011 projects incl. 5,360 IT) was seen as SNIPPET only (https://www.wrh.ox.ac.uk/publications/publication_modal/2243583).

### 3. Nucleus Research, "Quantifying Returns from Increased Productivity", Research Note C41, Feb 2007 (READ)

- URL: https://nucleusresearch.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/05/c41-Quantifying-productivity-returns.pdf (the www. host failed a certificate check; the bare host worked; PDF came back binary and I extracted text with pypdf; 3 pages).
- Quotes: "Productivity correction factor: A number less than 1 and more than 0 that is used to correct the estimate of time saved". Sales representatives: "a correction factor of .7 to .9". Line workers: "near 1". Marketing personnel: "a correction factor of .5 or less not unusual". "If you're not certain about the correction factor to apply, you can use .5 as an average or a more conservative measure." Also: corroborate with an independent measurement; use fully loaded cost (salary plus 35% as a starting point); count rollout/training time as a cost.
- Caveats: 2007 vendor research note, based on Nucleus's own case-study database (not published or peer-reviewed); no sample sizes. This is a recapture factor, not evidence-quality haircut. "Anything current" on role-based correction factors: I did not find a newer primary Nucleus publication; not found.

### 4. Time saved vs time recaptured

- Humlum and Vestergaard, "Large Language Models, Small Labor Market Effects", NBER WP 33777 / BFI WP 2025-56 (READ, BFI PDF text). URLs: https://bfi.uchicago.edu/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/BFI_WP_2025-56-3.pdf ; https://www.nber.org/papers/w33777 (abstract read). Quotes: "users report average time savings of 2.8% of work hours" vs "significant productivity gains - often exceeding 15% - documented by RCTs"; "only 3-7% of workers' productivity gains are passed through to higher earnings"; no significant effect on earnings or recorded hours, intervals ruling out effects larger than 1% (BFI May 2025 version; the NBER abstract text I fetched says 2% two years after launch, so check the version before citing); time savings reported by 64%-90% of users; reported savings about 7% where employers encourage use; adopters actively use chatbots only 5-6% of work hours on usage days. Sample: about 25,000 workers, 7,000 workplaces, 11 occupations in Denmark, 2023-24. Caveat: savings are self-reported; Denmark; chatbots only.
- METR, "Measuring the Impact of Early-2025 AI on Experienced Open-Source Developer Productivity" (READ page), https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/ . 16 developers, 246 issues, randomized. Developers expected 24% speedup; observed 19% longer with AI; afterwards still believed 20% faster. Caveats listed by METR: small sample, representativeness, mature familiar repos only, learning effects, and the page itself states the results are out of date (superseded by a Feb 2026 follow-up that I did not fetch).
- UK Government, Microsoft 365 Copilot trial press release (READ), https://www.gov.uk/government/news/landmark-government-trial-shows-ai-could-save-civil-servants-nearly-2-weeks-a-year . 20,000+ civil servants, 26 min/day average, "derived from self-reported daily time savings". Secondary coverage (SNIPPET, theregister/publictechnology): 17% reported no saving; tax profession 11 min vs project delivery 35 min. Caveats: self-reported, vendor-involved, annual figure extrapolated.
- Gap: I did not find a study that directly states "X% of time saved is recaptured as productive work" with a measured denominator. The closest are Nucleus (0.5-0.9 judgment factors) and the Danish pass-through (3-7% to earnings, which is a different quantity). An St. Louis Fed estimate I had in mind was not found by search and is NOT cited.
